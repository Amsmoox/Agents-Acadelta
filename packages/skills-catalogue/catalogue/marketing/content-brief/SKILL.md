# Content brief

The brief is where a piece is won or lost. A vague brief produces a piece nobody
wanted, and the revision costs more than the writing did.

## When to use

Commissioning or planning any substantial piece of content.

## What a brief contains

1. **The reader.** Who, what they already know, what they are trying to do.
   "Marketers" is not a reader; "a marketer at a 20-person company who has never
   run a paid campaign" is.
2. **The one question the piece answers.** If there are three, it is three pieces.
3. **What the reader can do differently afterwards.** A piece that changes nothing
   is entertainment, which is a valid goal but a different one.
4. **The angle** — why this piece rather than the ten that already exist. Read
   the top results before writing the brief; if you cannot say what yours adds,
   do not commission it.
5. **The structure** — the sections, in order, with a line on each.
6. **Evidence required** — the data, examples, or sources that must appear.
   Content that asserts without evidence is content nobody links to.
7. **Constraints** — length, tone, what to avoid, who reviews it.

## What to leave out

Do not write the piece in the brief. Specifying every sentence produces a worse
result than specifying the shape and letting the writer write.

## Distribution before commissioning

Decide where it will be published and how people will find it before it is
written. A good piece nobody reads is a failure of planning, not of writing.
